# Comprehensive Lecture Notes: Matrix Bases & Spanning Sets in $\mathbb{R}^3$

## 1. Overview
Building upon the formal definition of a **basis** (a set of vectors that is both **linearly independent** and **spans** the vector space), this section extends these concepts to two fundamental structures:
1. **Matrix Spaces:** Specifically $M_{2 \times 2}$, the vector space of all $2 \times 2$ real matrices.
2. **Three-Dimensional Euclidean Space ($\mathbb{R}^3$):** Using matrix determinants and **Cramer's Rule** as a primary algebraic method to test for linear independence, verify spanning properties, and find unique linear combinations.

---

## 2. Standard Basis for $M_{2 \times 2}$ Matrix Space

The vector space $M_{2 \times 2}$ consists of all $2 \times 2$ real matrices:
$$ M_{2 \times 2} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$

The standard (canonical) basis for $M_{2 \times 2}$ is composed of four matrices $E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22}$, where each matrix has a $1$ in a single specific entry and $0$ everywhere else:
$$ B = \left\{ E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22} \right\} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\} $$

### Complete Proof: $B$ is a Basis for $M_{2 \times 2}$

#### Step 1: Proof of Linear Independence
Consider the zero matrix equation with real scalars $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \alpha_4 \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$ \alpha_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \alpha_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \alpha_3 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \alpha_4 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

Perform scalar multiplication across each matrix:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \alpha_2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ \alpha_3 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \alpha_4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

Perform matrix addition by adding corresponding entries:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1 & \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3 & \alpha_4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

By matrix equality, comparing entry by entry directly yields:
$$ \alpha_1 = 0, \quad \alpha_2 = 0, \quad \alpha_3 = 0, \quad \alpha_4 = 0 $$

Since the only solution is the trivial solution ($\alpha_i = 0$ for all $i$), the set $B$ is **linearly independent**.

#### Step 2: Proof of Spanning Property
Let $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ be an arbitrary matrix in $M_{2 \times 2}$.

We can express this matrix as a sum of four component matrices:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ c & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix} $$

Factoring out the real scalars $a, b, c, d$:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = a \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + b \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + c \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + d \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

Since every arbitrary matrix in $M_{2 \times 2}$ can be written as a linear combination of the matrices in $B$, the set $B$ **spans $M_{2 \times 2}$** ($L(B) = M_{2 \times 2}$).

#### Conclusion
Because $B$ is linearly independent and spans $M_{2 \times 2}$, it is a **basis for $M_{2 \times 2}$**. The dimension of $M_{2 \times 2}$ is $4$.

## 3. Testing a Basis in $\mathbb{R}^3$ via Determinants & Cramer's Rule

When given a set of $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can place those vectors as the columns (or rows) of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$. 
- If the determinant $\det(A) \neq 0$, the vectors are **linearly independent**, span $\mathbb{R}^n$, and form a **basis**.
- **Cramer's Rule** can then be used to explicitly find the scalar coefficients for expressing any vector in terms of this basis.

---

### Worked Problem & Step-by-Step Exercise

**Problem Statement:**
1. Test whether the set $B = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\} = \{(1, 1, 0), (3, 0, 1), (5, 2, 2)\}$ forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$.
2. If it forms a basis, represent the vector $v = (1, 2, 3)$ as a linear combination of these basis vectors.

---

#### Step 1: Basis Test via Coefficient Matrix Determinant
Form the matrix $A$ by arranging the vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ as columns:
$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} $$

Calculate the determinant $D = \det(A)$ by expanding along the first row:
$$ D = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} - 3 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix} + 5 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} $$

Compute the $2 \times 2$ determinants:
- $\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = (0)(2) - (2)(1) = -2$
- $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(2) - (2)(0) = 2$
- $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(1) - (0)(0) = 1$

Substitute these back into the expansion:
$$ D = 1(-2) - 3(2) + 5(1) = -2 - 6 + 5 = -3 $$

Since $D = -3 \neq 0$:
1. The vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ are **linearly independent**.
2. Since there are 3 linearly independent vectors in a 3-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), the set $B$ also **spans $\mathbb{R}^3$**.
3. Therefore, $B = \{(1, 1, 0), (3, 0, 1), (5, 2, 2)\}$ **forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$**.

---

#### Step 2: System Setup for Arbitrary Vector Representation
Let $(a, b, c)$ be an arbitrary vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$. We want to find scalar coefficients $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ such that:
$$ \alpha_1 (1, 1, 0) + \alpha_2 (3, 0, 1) + \alpha_3 (5, 2, 2) = (a, b, c) $$

Expanding component-wise gives the system of linear equations:
$$ \begin{aligned}
\alpha_1 + 3\alpha_2 + 5\alpha_3 &= a \\
\alpha_1 + 0\alpha_2 + 2\alpha_3 &= b \\
0\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + 2\alpha_3 &= c
\end{aligned} $$

In matrix form $A X = C$:
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} $$

---

#### Step 3: Solving for General Coefficients $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ using Cramer's Rule

Cramer's Rule states that $\alpha_i = \frac{D_i}{D}$, where $D_i$ is the determinant of matrix $A$ with its $i^{\text{th}}$ column replaced by the constants vector $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix}$.

##### Computing $\alpha_1$:
Replace column 1 with $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix}$:
$$ D_1 = \begin{vmatrix} a & 3 & 5 \\ b & 0 & 2 \\ c & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} $$

Expand along the second column:
$$ D_1 = -3 \begin{vmatrix} b & 2 \\ c & 2 \end{vmatrix} + 0 \begin{vmatrix} a & 5 \\ c & 2 \end{vmatrix} - 1 \begin{vmatrix} a & 5 \\ b & 2 \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ D_1 = -3(2b - 2c) - 1(2a - 5b) = -6b + 6c - 2a + 5b = -2a - b + 6c $$

Thus:
$$ \alpha_1 = \frac{D_1}{D} = \frac{-2a - b + 6c}{-3} $$

##### Computing $\alpha_2$:
Replace column 2 with $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix}$:
$$ D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & a & 5 \\ 1 & b & 2 \\ 0 & c & 2 \end{vmatrix} $$

Expand along the first column:
$$ D_2 = 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} b & 2 \\ c & 2 \end{vmatrix} - 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} a & 5 \\ c & 2 \end{vmatrix} + 0 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} a & 5 \\ b & 2 \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ D_2 = (2b - 2c) - (2a - 5c) = 2b - 2c - 2a + 5c = -2a + 2b + 3c $$

Thus:
$$ \alpha_2 = \frac{D_2}{D} = \frac{-2a + 2b + 3c}{-3} $$

##### Computing $\alpha_3$:
Replace column 3 with $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix}$:
$$ D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 & a \\ 1 & 0 & b \\ 0 & 1 & c \end{vmatrix} $$

Expand along the first column:
$$ D_3 = 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 0 & b \\ 1 & c \end{vmatrix} - 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 3 & a \\ 1 & c \end{vmatrix} + 0 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 3 & a \\ 0 & b \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ D_3 = (0 - b) - (3c - a) = -b - 3c + a = a - b - 3c $$

Thus:
$$ \alpha_3 = \frac{D_3}{D} = \frac{a - b - 3c}{-3} $$

---

#### Step 4: Representing $(1, 2, 3)$ in Terms of the Basis
To find the explicit representation of $(a, b, c) = (1, 2, 3)$, substitute $a = 1$, $b = 2$, and $c = 3$ into the formulas for $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$:

1. **Calculate $\alpha_1$:**
   $$ \alpha_1 = \frac{-2(1) - (2) + 6(3)}{-3} = \frac{-2 - 2 + 18}{-3} = \frac{14}{-3} = -\frac{14}{3} $$

2. **Calculate $\alpha_2$:**
   $$ \alpha_2 = \frac{-2(1) + 2(2) + 3(3)}{-3} = \frac{-2 + 4 + 9}{-3} = \frac{11}{-3} = -\frac{11}{3} $$

3. **Calculate $\alpha_3$:**
   $$ \alpha_3 = \frac{1 - 2 - 3(3)}{-3} = \frac{1 - 2 - 9}{-3} = \frac{-10}{-3} = \frac{10}{3} $$

#### Final Result:
The vector $(1, 2, 3)$ is uniquely expressed as a linear combination of the basis vectors as follows:
$$ (1, 2, 3) = -\frac{14}{3}(1, 1, 0) - \frac{11}{3}(3, 0, 1) + \frac{10}{3}(5, 2, 2) $$

---

### Verification of the Solution
To ensure accuracy, substitute the coefficients back into the linear combination:
$$ -\frac{14}{3}(1, 1, 0) - \frac{11}{3}(3, 0, 1) + \frac{10}{3}(5, 2, 2) $$
$$ = \left( -\frac{14}{3} - \frac{33}{3} + \frac{50}{3}, \; -\frac{14}{3} + 0 + \frac{20}{3}, \; 0 - \frac{11}{3} + \frac{20}{3} \right) $$
$$ = \left( \frac{-14 - 33 + 50}{3}, \; \frac{-14 + 20}{3}, \; \frac{-11 + 20}{3} \right) $$
$$ = \left( \frac{3}{3}, \; \frac{6}{3}, \; \frac{9}{3} \right) = (1, 2, 3) $$

The solution is verified to be fully correct.
