# Section 5.3: Vectors, Euclidean Space $E^n$, and Matrix Vector Spaces

Building upon the general algebraic definition of a vector space, we now focus on specific, concrete vector spaces used across engineering disciplines: **$n$-component column/row vectors**, **$n$-dimensional Euclidean space ($E^n$)**, and **spaces of matrices ($M_{m \times n}$)**.

---

## 1. Fundamentals of $n$-Component Vectors

In applied engineering and linear algebra, vectors are often represented as ordered lists of numbers.

> **Definition (Vector Structure):** An **$n$-component vector** $\mathbf{a}$ is an ordered $n$-tuple of real numbers. It can be written in two standard formats:
> 
> * **Row Vector:**
>   $$ \mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) $$
> 
> * **Column Vector:**
>   $$ \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix} $$
> 
> The real numbers $a_i$ (for $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$) are called the **components** (or entries) of the vector.

---

### 1.1 Special Vector Types

In operational math, optimization, and signal processing, several specific types of vectors recur frequently:

1. **Unit Vectors ($\mathbf{e}_i$):**
   A unit vector $\mathbf{e}_i$ has a value of $1$ (unity) at its $i^{\text{th}}$ component and $0$ for all other components.
   $$ \mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \dots, \quad \mathbf{e}_n = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

2. **Null Vector (Zero Vector, $\mathbf{0}$):**
   A vector in which every single component is zero:
   $$ \mathbf{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

3. **Sum Vector ($\mathbf{1}$):**
   A vector in which every component is equal to unity ($1$):
   $$ \mathbf{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

---

### 1.2 Vector Operations & Equality

#### Vector Equality
> Two $n$-component vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are **equal** ($\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b}$) if and only if their corresponding components are strictly equal:
> $$ a_i = b_i \quad \text{for all } i = 1, 2, \dots, n $$

#### Scalar (Dot) Product
> Given two $n$-component vectors $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$, their **scalar product** (also known as the inner product or dot product $\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle$) yields a single scalar value:
> $$ \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i $$

#### Norm (Magnitude) of a Vector
> The **norm** (or length/magnitude) of an $n$-component vector $\mathbf{a}$ is denoted by $\|\mathbf{a}\|$ and is defined using the scalar product of $\mathbf{a}$ with itself:
> $$ \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{\langle\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{a}\rangle} = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \dots + a_n^2} $$

## 2. $n$-Dimensional Euclidean Space ($E^n$)

Euclidean space $E^n$ provides the standard framework for geometry and physics in $n$ dimensions.

> **Definition ($E^n$):** An **$n$-dimensional Euclidean space** $E^n$ is the collection of all ordered $n$-tuples of real (or complex) numbers:
> $$ E^n = \{ (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) \mid a_i \in \mathbb{R} \text{ or } \mathbb{C}, \, 1 \le i \le n \} $$

### 2.1 Vector Space Operations in $E^n$

To verify that $E^n$ forms a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$, we define two core operations component-wise:

1. **Vector Addition:**
   For any $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) \in E^n$ and $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n) \in E^n$:
   $$ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1, \, a_2 + b_2, \, \dots, \, a_n + b_n) \in E^n $$

2. **Scalar Multiplication:**
   For any scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) \in E^n$:
   $$ \lambda \mathbf{a} = (\lambda a_1, \, \lambda a_2, \, \dots, \, \lambda a_n) \in E^n $$

Because component-wise addition and scalar multiplication satisfy all ten vector space axioms (closure, associativity, identity, inverse, commutativity, and distributivity properties inherited from real numbers), **$E^n$ is a formal Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$**.

---

## 3. Matrix Vector Spaces ($M_{2 \times 2}$)

Vector spaces are not limited to ordered lists of numbers ($E^n$). Sets of matrices also satisfy the formal definition of a vector space.

> **Example ($M_{2 \times 2}$ Vector Space):**  
> Consider the set $M_{2 \times 2}$ of all $2 \times 2$ matrices with real entries:
> $$ M_{2 \times 2} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
> 
> With standard matrix addition and scalar multiplication defined as:
> $$ \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \\ b_3 & b_4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 + b_1 & a_2 + b_2 \\ a_3 + b_3 & a_4 + b_4 \end{pmatrix} $$
> $$ \lambda \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda a_1 & \lambda a_2 \\ \lambda a_3 & \lambda a_4 \end{pmatrix} $$
> 
> $M_{2 \times 2}$ forms a valid **Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$**.

## 4. Step-by-Step Practical Problem Solving

### Example Problem 1: Scalar Product and Norm Calculation

**Problem Statement:**  
Given two vectors in $E^4$:
$$ \mathbf{a} = (2, -1, 0, 4), \quad \mathbf{b} = (1, 3, -2, 1) $$
1. Compute the scalar product $\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle$.
2. Compute the norm of vector $\mathbf{a}$ ($\|\mathbf{a}\|$).

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

1. **Calculate the Scalar Product $\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle$:**
   Apply the formula $\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{4} a_i b_i$:
   $$ \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = (2)(1) + (-1)(3) + (0)(-2) + (4)(1) $$
   $$ \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = 2 - 3 + 0 + 4 = 3 $$

2. **Calculate the Norm $\|\mathbf{a}\|$:**
   Apply the norm formula $\|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 + a_4^2}$:
   $$ \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 4^2} $$
   $$ \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 0 + 16} = \sqrt{21} $$

### Example Problem 2: Verifying Closure and Identity in $M_{2 \times 2}$

**Problem Statement:**  
Demonstrate that the additive identity element exists in $M_{2 \times 2}$ and verify closure under scalar multiplication for $\lambda = -3$ and $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

1. **Existence of Additive Identity:**
   The zero vector in $M_{2 \times 2}$ is the zero matrix $\mathbf{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
   For any matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} \in M_{2 \times 2}$:
   $$ A + \mathbf{0} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 + 0 & a_2 + 0 \\ a_3 + 0 & a_4 + 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ a_3 & a_4 \end{pmatrix} = A $$
   Thus, the zero matrix serves as the unique additive identity.

2. **Verification of Scalar Multiplication Closure:**
   Multiply $A$ by $\lambda = -3$:
   $$ -3 A = -3 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -3(1) & -3(-2) \\ -3(4) & -3(0) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 6 \\ -12 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$
   Since all resulting entries $(-3, 6, -12, 0)$ are real numbers, the result is an element of $M_{2 \times 2}$, confirming closure under scalar multiplication.
