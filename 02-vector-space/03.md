## 1. Vector Subspaces

### 1.1 Conceptual Motivation & Definition
In linear algebra, we frequently encounter smaller subsets within a larger vector space that retain all the algebraic structures of the parent space. Rather than constructing a brand-new vector space from scratch, we can look at a subset $W$ inside a known vector space $V$ and test whether $W$ itself forms a vector space under the exact same addition and scalar multiplication operations.

```
                  +---------------------------------------+
                  |       Parent Vector Space V           |
                  |                                       |
                  |   +-------------------------------+   |
                  |   |    Subspace W                 |   |
                  |   |  - Contains 0-vector          |   |
                  |   |  - Closed under addition      |   |
                  |   |  - Closed under scalar mult.  |   |
                  |   +-------------------------------+   |
                  +---------------------------------------+

```

![Diagram: Subspace W embedded within a larger Vector Space V](./images/vector_space_subspace.png)
*The visual above demonstrates a subset $W$ inside a larger vector space $V$. For $W$ to be a subspace, any vector addition or scalar multiplication performed on elements inside $W$ must remain entirely inside $W$.*

> **Definition (Subspace):** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $W \subseteq V$ be a non-empty subset of $V$. Then $W$ is called a **subspace** of $V$ if $W$ is itself a vector space over $F$ under the same operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication defined on $V$.

To verify whether a subset $W$ is a vector space using the standard definition, one would theoretically need to verify all 8 vector space axioms (associativity, commutativity, existence of zero vector, existence of additive inverses, and scalar multiplication properties). However, because $W$ inherits most properties directly from $V$, we can drastically simplify this verification process.

---

### 1.2 The Two-Condition Subspace Test Theorem
Instead of checking all 8 axioms, we can determine if $W$ is a subspace using a single, unified criterion known as the **Subspace Test**.

> **Theorem (Subspace Criterion):** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $W$ be a non-empty subset of $V$ ($\emptyset \neq W \subseteq V$). Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if for all scalars $\alpha, \beta \in F$ and all vectors $w_1, w_2 \in W$, the linear combination $\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2$ is also in $W$:
> 
> $$\alpha, \beta \in F \text{ and } w_1, w_2 \in W \implies \alpha w_1 + \beta w_2 \in W$$

#### Detailed Proof
To establish that statement (i) "$W$ is a subspace of $V$" is equivalent to statement (ii) "$\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2 \in W$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in F, w_1, w_2 \in W$", we must prove both directions ($\implies$ and $\impliedby$).

**Direction 1: Direct Proof $(\text{i} \implies \text{ii})$**
1. Assume $W$ is a subspace of $V$.
2. By definition, $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition.
3. Given $w_1, w_2 \in W$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$:
   - Since $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication, $\alpha w_1 \in W$ and $\beta w_2 \in W$.
   - Since $W$ is closed under vector addition, $(\alpha w_1) + (\beta w_2) \in W$.
4. Thus, $\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2 \in W$.

**Direction 2: Converse Proof $(\text{ii} \implies \text{i})$**
1. Assume $W$ is a non-empty subset of $V$ such that $\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2 \in W$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in F$ and $w_1, w_2 \in W$.
2. **Closure under Addition:** Choose $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 1$. Then $1 \cdot w_1 + 1 \cdot w_2 = w_1 + w_2 \in W$. Hence, $W$ is closed under addition.
3. **Closure under Scalar Multiplication:** Choose $\beta = 0$ (or $w_2 = w_1$) and any $\alpha \in F$. Then $\alpha w_1 + 0 \cdot w_2 = \alpha w_1 \in W$. Hence, $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication.
4. **Existence of Zero Vector:** Since $W$ is non-empty, pick any vector $w \in W$. Choosing $\alpha = 0$ gives $0 \cdot w = \mathbf{0} \in W$.
5. **Existence of Additive Inverse:** For any vector $w \in W$, choose $\alpha = -1$. Then $(-1)w = -w \in W$.
6. **Inherited Axioms:** Associativity, commutativity, and scalar distributive properties hold automatically for all vectors in $W$ because they hold for all vectors in the ambient space $V$.

Therefore, $W$ is a vector space under the operations of $V$, making it a subspace of $V$. $\blacksquare$

### 1.3 Concrete Examples of Subspaces

#### Example 1: Matrix Subspace $S_{2 \times 2}$ in $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$
Let $M_{2 \times 2}$ be the vector space of all $2 \times 2$ real matrices. Consider the subset $S_{2 \times 2}$ defined as:

$$S_{2 \times 2} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & a \\ a & a \end{pmatrix} \Bigg| a \in \mathbb{R} \right\}$$

We apply the Subspace Test to verify whether $S_{2 \times 2}$ is a subspace of $M_{2 \times 2}$.

1. **Non-emptiness:** Choose $a = 0$. The zero matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in S_{2 \times 2}$, so $S_{2 \times 2} \neq \emptyset$.
2. **Linear Combination Test:** Let $A, B \in S_{2 \times 2}$ and let $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$.
   By definition of $S_{2 \times 2}$, there exist real numbers $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
   $$A = \begin{pmatrix} x & x \\ x & x \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} y & y \\ y & y \end{pmatrix}$$
3. Compute $\alpha A + \beta B$:
   $$\alpha A + \beta B = \alpha \begin{pmatrix} x & x \\ x & x \end{pmatrix} + \beta \begin{pmatrix} y & y \\ y & y \end{pmatrix}$$
   $$= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha x & \alpha x \\ \alpha x & \alpha x \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} \beta y & \beta y \\ \beta y & \beta y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha x + \beta y & \alpha x + \beta y \\ \alpha x + \beta y & \alpha x + \beta y \end{pmatrix}$$
4. Let $k = \alpha x + \beta y$. Since $\alpha, \beta, x, y \in \mathbb{R}$, $k$ is a real number ($k \in \mathbb{R}$).
5. Therefore, $\alpha A + \beta B = \begin{pmatrix} k & k \\ k & k \end{pmatrix} \in S_{2 \times 2}$.

By the Subspace Test, $S_{2 \times 2}$ is a subspace of $M_{2 \times 2}$.

#### Example 2: One-Dimensional Line Subspace in $\mathbb{R}^3$
Consider the set $W = \{\alpha(1, 1, 0) \mid \alpha \in \mathbb{R}\}$ inside the Euclidean vector space $\mathbb{R}^3$. Geometrically, $W$ represents a line passing through the origin $(0,0,0)$ in 3D space.

![Diagram: Line passing through origin in R3](./images/line_subspace_r3.png)
*The line $W = \{\alpha(1, 1, 0)\}$ passes directly through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Any scalar multiple or sum of vectors along this line stays on the line.*

**Proof using Subspace Criterion:**
1. **Non-emptiness:** Setting $\alpha = 0$ gives $0(1,1,0) = (0,0,0) \in W$. Thus $W$ is non-empty.
2. **Linear Combination Test:** Pick $w_1, w_2 \in W$ and $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$.
   Since $w_1, w_2 \in W$, there exist scalars $r, s \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
   $$w_1 = r(1,1,0), \quad w_2 = s(1,1,0)$$
3. Form the combination $c w_1 + d w_2$:
   $$c w_1 + d w_2 = c[r(1,1,0)] + d[s(1,1,0)]$$
   $$= (cr)(1,1,0) + (ds)(1,1,0)$$
   $$= (cr + ds)(1,1,0)$$
4. Set $k = cr + ds \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $c w_1 + d w_2 = k(1,1,0) \in W$.

Thus, $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## 2. Linear Combinations

### 2.1 Formal Definition
A linear combination is the basic operational building block of linear algebra. It combines vector addition and scalar multiplication into a single expression.

> **Definition (Linear Combination):** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$. Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ be a finite collection of vectors, and let $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in F$ be scalars. An expression of the form:
> 
> $$\alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i$$
> 
> is called a **linear combination** of the vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ over the field $F$.

---

### 2.2 Numerical Worked Example ($\mathbb{R}^4$)
**Problem:** Given the vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$:
$$\mathbf{a} = (2, 3, 4, 7), \quad \mathbf{b} = (0, 0, 0, 1), \quad \mathbf{c} = (1, 0, 1, 0)$$
Verify that the vector $\mathbf{d} = (5, 3, 7, 9)$ is a linear combination of $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$.

**Solution:**
We seek scalars $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
$$\mathbf{d} = \alpha_1 \mathbf{a} + \alpha_2 \mathbf{b} + \alpha_3 \mathbf{c}$$

Substitute the vector component forms:
$$(5, 3, 7, 9) = \alpha_1 (2, 3, 4, 7) + \alpha_2 (0, 0, 0, 1) + \alpha_3 (1, 0, 1, 0)$$

Expand and group by components:
$$(5, 3, 7, 9) = (2\alpha_1 + \alpha_3, \; 3\alpha_1, \; 4\alpha_1 + \alpha_3, \; 7\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)$$

This yields a system of 4 linear equations:
$$\begin{aligned}
(1) \quad 2\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 &= 5 \\
(2) \quad 3\alpha_1 &= 3 \\
(3) \quad 4\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 &= 7 \\
(4) \quad 7\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 &= 9
\end{aligned}$$

**Step 1:** Solve equation (2) for $\alpha_1$:
$$3\alpha_1 = 3 \implies \alpha_1 = 1$$

**Step 2:** Substitute $\alpha_1 = 1$ into equation (1):
$$2(1) + \alpha_3 = 5 \implies \alpha_3 = 3$$

**Step 3:** Substitute $\alpha_1 = 1$ into equation (4):
$$7(1) + \alpha_2 = 9 \implies \alpha_2 = 2$$

**Step 4:** Consistency Check on equation (3):
$$4\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 = 4(1) + 3 = 7$$
The value matches equation (3) perfectly.

**Final Linear Combination:**
$$(5, 3, 7, 9) = 1\mathbf{a} + 2\mathbf{b} + 3\mathbf{c} = (2, 3, 4, 7) + 2(0, 0, 0, 1) + 3(1, 0, 1, 0)$$

### 2.3 Canonical Basis Linear Combination in $\mathbb{R}^3$
Consider Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$ over the field $\mathbb{R}$ and the standard basis vectors:
$$e_1 = (1, 0, 0), \quad e_2 = (0, 1, 0), \quad e_3 = (0, 0, 1)$$

Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ be an arbitrary vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$. We can express $\mathbf{x}$ as a linear combination of $e_1, e_2, e_3$:

$$(x_1, x_2, x_3) = x_1(1, 0, 0) + x_2(0, 1, 0) + x_3(0, 0, 1) = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2 + x_3 e_3$$

Because every vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be uniquely written as a linear combination of $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$, this set spans $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## 3. The Linear Span $L(S)$

### 3.1 Formal Definition
When we take a set of vectors $S$ and collect every possible linear combination that can be formed from them, we create a new set called the **linear span** of $S$.

> **Definition (Linear Span):** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S \subseteq V$ be a subset of $V$. The **linear span** of $S$, denoted $L(S)$ or $\text{Span}(S)$, is the set of all finite linear combinations of elements of $S$:
> 
> $$L(S) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i \Bigg| n \in \mathbb{N}, \; v_i \in S, \; \alpha_i \in F \text{ for } 1 \leq i \leq n \right\}$$
> 
> If $S = \emptyset$, we define $L(\emptyset) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

---

### 3.2 Property: Containment $S \subseteq L(S)$
For any non-empty set of vectors $S \subseteq V$, the original set is always a subset of its span.

**Proof:**
1. Let $v \in S$.
2. Choose $n = 1$, $v_1 = v$, and the field scalar $\alpha_1 = 1 \in F$.
3. Form the linear combination:
   $$1 \cdot v = v$$
4. Since $v$ is written as a linear combination of an element from $S$, $v \in L(S)$.
5. Since every element $v \in S$ is also in $L(S)$, it follows that:
   $$S \subseteq L(S)$$

---

### 3.3 Theorem & Proof: $L(S)$ is a Subspace
The linear span of *any* arbitrary subset $S$ automatically forms a valid vector subspace of $V$.

> **Theorem:** If $S$ is any subset of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$, then $L(S)$ is a subspace of $V$.

```
                  +-----------------------------------------+
                  |         Vector Space V                  |
                  |                                         |
                  |    +---------------+                    |
                  |    | Set S         |                    |
                  |    | {v1, v2}      |                    |
                  |    +-------+-------+                    |
                  |            |                            |
                  |            v (all linear combinations)  |
                  |    +-------------------------------+    |
                  |    | Linear Span L(S)              |    |
                  |    | (Forming a Subspace)          |    |
                  |    +-------------------------------+    |
                  +-----------------------------------------+

```

![Diagram: Set S inside Linear Span L(S) inside Vector Space V](./images/linear_span_subspace.png)
*Visualizing $S \subseteq L(S) \subseteq V$. Taking all linear combinations of vectors in $S$ fills out the entire subspace $L(S)$.*

#### Complete Step-by-Step Proof
To prove that $L(S)$ is a subspace of $V$, we use the Subspace Criterion Theorem proved in Section 1.2.

**Step 1: Verify $L(S)$ is non-empty.**
Since $S$ is a subset of $V$, if $S = \emptyset$, $L(\emptyset) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, which is the zero subspace. If $S \neq \emptyset$, pick $v \in S$. Then $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0} \in L(S)$. Thus, $L(S) \neq \emptyset$.

**Step 2: Select arbitrary vectors in $L(S)$ and arbitrary scalars.**
Let $v, w \in L(S)$ and let $\alpha, \beta \in F$.

**Step 3: Express $v$ and $w$ as finite linear combinations of vectors in $S$.**
By definition of $L(S)$, there exist vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in S$ and scalars $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in F$ such that:
$$v = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i$$

Similarly, there exist vectors $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_m \in S$ and scalars $\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_m \in F$ such that:
$$w = \beta_1 w_1 + \beta_2 w_2 + \dots + \beta_m w_m = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \beta_j w_j$$

**Step 4: Form the linear combination $\alpha v + \beta w$.**
$$\alpha v + \beta w = \alpha \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i \right) + \beta \left( \sum_{j=1}^{m} \beta_j w_j \right)$$

Apply the distributive axiom of vector spaces:
$$\alpha v + \beta w = \left( \alpha \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha \alpha_n v_n \right) + \left( \beta \beta_1 w_1 + \beta \beta_2 w_2 + \dots + \beta \beta_m w_m \right)$$

**Step 5: Analyze the resulting expression.**
- The terms $(\alpha \alpha_i)$ and $(\beta \beta_j)$ are products of field elements in $F$. Since fields are closed under multiplication, $(\alpha \alpha_i) \in F$ and $(\beta \beta_j) \in F$.
- The vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n, w_1, \dots, w_m$ all belong to $S$.
- The expression $\alpha v + \beta w$ is a finite sum of elements of $S$ multiplied by scalars in $F$.

Therefore, by definition of linear span:
$$\alpha v + \beta w \in L(S)$$

**Conclusion:**
By the Subspace Criterion, $L(S)$ is a subspace of $V$. $\blacksquare$

## 4. Comprehensive Summary & Concepts Comparison

| Concept | Symbolic Representation | Intuitive Geometric / Algebraic Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Vector Subspace** | $W \subseteq V$ | A self-contained vector space nested inside a larger vector space $V$ using the same operations. |
| **Linear Combination** | $\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i v_i$ | Scaling a finite set of vectors and adding them together. |
| **Linear Span** | $L(S)$ or $\text{Span}(S)$ | The set of *all* possible vectors reachable by taking linear combinations of vectors in $S$. Always forms a subspace. |
